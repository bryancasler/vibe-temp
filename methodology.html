How Dogs mode works

Dogs mode is an optional layer on Vibe Temp for people walking a golden retriever. It is off until you turn it on, and your browser remembers the choice. When it is on, the chart gets a paw for the best time to get a golden out on the grass, a walk rating for every hour, and six cards for heat, sunny pavement, storms, air quality, salt and cold.

Most of it is ported from DC Goldens' Dog Weather. This page is every rule behind it: the number that sets off each line, whose number it is, how much weight its source can bear, and what was left out on purpose. The short version: there is no heat index or pavement temperature for dogs with evidence behind it, and the one air quality scale for pets is the EPA's, adapted. Where a scale exists for people, Dogs mode uses it and says so. Where a line is someone's own choice, it is marked Ours.

This is guidance, not veterinary advice. Every golden is different, and the thresholds behind these are thinner than you would hope: there is no published heat index for dogs, and nobody has measured what temperature burns a paw. When in doubt, ask your vet, and watch your dog rather than the number.

Sun and shade

The two lines on the chart, In the sun and In the shade, show how warm it is likely to feel to a person standing in full sun, and to the same person in shade. They are Bryan Casler's own estimate for Vibe Temp, not a published formula. He set its numbers by hand until they matched how it actually feels outside. They are there with Dogs mode on or off.

In the shade starts with the air temperature, adds 1°F for every 15 points of humidity above 40% (and takes 1°F off for every 15 points below it), and takes 0.7°F off for every mile an hour of wind. In the sun adds up to 8°F on top of that, depending on how much sun gets through, plus 1.2°F for heat bouncing back off the ground whenever any does. How much sun gets through comes from the forecast: the UV index against what it would be under a clear sky, dimmed by how much of the sky counts as cloud. At night, and under a sky that lets no sun through, the two lines are the same.

In the shade = T + (RH - 40) / 15 - 0.7 × W In the sun = In the shade + 8 × S + 4 × R, when S is above 0 S = (UV / clear-sky UV) × (1 - (C / 100)^0.7), kept between 0 and 1, and 0 at night C = 100 × (1 - (direct sunlight / all sunlight) / 0.85), kept between 0 and 100

T is the air temperature in °F, RH the relative humidity in %, W the wind speed in mph, R the ground's reflectivity (0.3 for concrete, so 4 × 0.3 = 1.2°F), and C the share of the sky that counts as cloud. Night is each hour's own is_day flag from the forecast. Without a clear-sky UV reading, S uses the UV index over 10. These are the default settings; Vibe Temp's calibration settings can change them, and the lines follow.

C comes from how much of the sunlight arrives as a direct beam, rather than the forecast's cloud percentage. So thin high cloud with the sun plainly out still counts as sun, and the low sun just after sunrise and before sunset counts for less. A daylight hour that reads no sunlight at all counts as full cloud, so the two lines meet. Only when the forecast has no sunlight reading does C go back to the forecast's cloud cover. The 0.85 is a first guess from DC's clear middays in 2025.

It describes a person, not a dog: nobody has measured how a golden's coat changes what the sun feels like, so Dogs mode doesn't turn it into a dog's number. The paw-to-grass time applies a range chosen for a golden to the sun line, and says so. It is not a pavement temperature, and it is not the wind chill. And it does not decide the walk ratings: those come from the National Weather Service's heat index, the air quality, storms, rain, sunny pavement, the air temperature and DC's Extreme Cold Alert level, as described below. On a hot, humid afternoon the heat index can read higher than even the sun line; they are different scales. The National Weather Service says full sun can add up to 15°F to the heat index; the sun line sits at most 9.2°F above the shade line at these settings.

Two best times

The chart can mark two times to get out on the grass, because the best time is different for a person and for a golden: a leaf for people, always, and a paw for Goldens, when Dogs mode is on.

🍃 Touch grass: Vibe Temp's rule for people

The leaf marks the best time for a person to get outside, by the rule Bryan wrote for Vibe Temp. It reads the In the sun line at points 15 minutes apart, and looks only at daylight points from 65 to 75°F (18 to 24°C when the page shows Celsius). Daylight goes by each hour's is_day flag from the forecast. Each point scores 100, less 10 for every degree it sits from the middle of the range (70°F, or 21°C), and a point from 10am to 4:45pm scores 20 more. The best score on each day wins, and a tie keeps the earlier time.

Where the sun line sits within 5% of the shade line, the rule reads the shade value, as it always has, so its picks stay where they were. No walk rule applies to it, with Dogs mode on or off: it is for people.

🐾 Paw to grass: DC Goldens' pick for a golden

The paw marks one time: the best time for a golden to get out on the grass. It sits on the chart's In the sun line, at the minute the line comes closest to 57°F (14°C), in daylight (from that day's sunrise to its sunset), from now on, and where the walk ratings call the hours on either side of it Good for a walk. The forecast gives one reading an hour, so a minute between two of them is read off the curve the chart draws through them: an estimate from the curve, no more exact than the two hours it joins. Anything from 45 to 64°F (7 to 18°C) can hold it, judged in whole degrees Fahrenheit whichever units the page shows, and a tie goes to the earlier minute. The 24-hour view has one paw, in the next 24 hours; the week view puts one on each day that has a time that fits. When nothing fits, there is no paw.

The range is DC Goldens' choice, from dcgoldens.org. No study sets one, and the researchers closest to the question say advice on safe temperatures for exercising dogs is missing. What the evidence does say points cooler than a person. A resting dog holds its temperature without effort from about 68 to 86°F (20 to 30°C), and a walk adds heat it mostly has to pant away. At UK canicross races where the air averaged 48°F (9°C), at least one running dog per race finished with an ear temperature above 105°F (40.6°C). And goldens had 2.67 times the odds of heat-related illness of Labradors. So DC Goldens aimed 13°F below the 70°F that Vibe Temp aims for a person, stopped the range at 64°F, and let it reach further on the cool side than the warm side, because the evidence of harm for goldens is on the warm side.

It scores the In the sun line, the warmer of the two, because a golden on open grass is usually in the sun. The UK study of dogs seen for heat-related illness read each day's weather at shaded weather stations, which its authors say likely understate what a dog meets; the canicross temperature above was measured on the course, out of the shade. Both lines estimate how it feels to a person; nobody has measured how a golden's coat takes the sun. The paw is a pleasant time for a walk, not a safe temperature and not a veterinary threshold: dogs in these studies overheated on mild days too.

Walk ratings

With Dogs mode on, a strip under the chart rates every hour, day and night, for a walk. The weather decides, not the clock. Each hour gets one of seven names, in three levels. Skips beat cautions, and cautions beat good; within the skips, too hot comes before too cold, then storms or air.

Which lines make a skip, which make a caution, and the 50% rain line are DC Goldens' choices, and so is borrowing DC's heat and cold alert levels, which are set for people, for a golden. The heat index

Dogs mode computes the heat index from the air temperature and humidity exactly as the National Weather Service's Weather Prediction Center publishes it: first the simple formula; if that comes to 80°F or more, the Rothfusz regression instead, with the Weather Service's adjustments for very dry air (humidity under 13% at 80 to 112°F) and very damp air (over 85% at 80 to 87°F). It is a scale made for people, in shade and light wind; no veterinary body publishes one for dogs. Open-Meteo's own "apparent temperature" is a different formula, so it is not used.

The wind chill, never printed

Dogs mode works out the National Weather Service's 2001 wind chill in one place only: to tell when an hour reaches DC's Extreme Cold Alert level, which the District writes in wind chill for people. It is only defined at 50°F or colder with wind above 3 mph; outside that it is the air temperature. It decides Too cold for Goldens and nothing else, and the page never shows it. Why, under What was left out.

The six dog cards

With Dogs mode on, a card shows only when its line is crossed, and each one names its sources, which link to the list below.

🌡️ Heat

The heat index, worked out as above, at the day's highest. It is a scale made for people, in shade; full sun can add up to 15°F. The dog evidence comes after it: goldens have 2.67 times the odds of heat-related illness that Labradors do, and most dog heatstroke follows exercise, usually an ordinary walk. On the UK's human heat-health alert days in 2022, emergency vets saw five times as many dogs with heatstroke a day as on other summer days; that is the UK's alert, and DC's Heat Alert level stands in for it here. The early signs are changes in breathing and lethargy, such as not wanting to go on.

☀️ Sunny pavement

Marks the daylight hours when pavement is likely sitting in hot sun, so grass and shade are the cooler way through. Sunlit surfaces have been measured 36 to 56°F hotter than the same material in shade at the same moment. The card never prints a pavement temperature, because nobody has measured what temperature burns a paw. The back-of-hand test is a fine habit; the published advice says 5, 7 or 10 seconds, and none of those counts was tested.

⛈️ Thunderstorms

Lead time, not a forecast of harm. Almost a third of dogs are sensitive to noise, and the things that help (a quiet spot, an earlier walk, a plan from your vet) all need warning. The storm line comes from the National Weather Service's own forecast for your area, looking 48 hours ahead, in the Weather Service's words: possible when it gives a chance of thunderstorms (30 to 50%), likely when it says likely (60% or more). A slight chance never shows. It covers the US only.

Why storms come from the National Weather Service. The forecast model behind the rest of the chart files DC's storm days under heavy showers: from January 2024 to September 2026 it never forecast an hour of thunder there, while Reagan National Airport recorded thunder on 119 days. So the storm line reads the National Weather Service's own forecast instead. Over May to September of 2025 and 2026, its Baltimore/Washington office gave at least a chance of thunderstorms a day ahead on 63 of the 69 days thunder was heard at the airport. It also gave a chance on about 40 days that stayed quiet there, which is what a 30 to 50% chance looks like, so the card says possible, not coming. The comparison is DC Goldens', and small: one airport, two summers and nothing from the colder months, scored against the Weather Service's archived forecasts for that airport.

💨 Air quality

The EPA's air quality index, labelled as the scale made for people that it is. At Good and Moderate the card shows the reading and nothing more. From Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups up, it adds Colorado State University's advice for pets, paraphrased and credited, and keeps their word: adapted. At Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups, that is less time outside for every dog, and bathroom breaks only for sensitive dogs (puppies, seniors, overweight dogs, and dogs with allergies or heart or lung trouble); at Unhealthy, limiting time outside and walking rather than running; above that, keeping dogs inside as much as you can. Mornings are usually better on ozone days.

🧂 Winter salt

A condition, not a temperature: salt goes down ahead of forecast snow and ice and stays down after, whatever the thermometer says. When there has been or will be snow or ice, the card says salt is probably down and suggests wiping or rinsing paws after the walk, before your golden licks them. Ice melt irritates paw pads and can poison a dog who eats enough of it.

❄️ Below freezing

DC law, not a vet chart. Below 32°F is "extreme weather" in the D.C. Code, when a dog can't be left outside for more than 15 minutes without a person or proper shelter unless its age, condition and type let it handle the cold. That rule is about dogs left out, not walks. On a walk, salt and ice are the usual winter hazards, and puppies, seniors and sick dogs keep warm less well. When some of its hours reach DC's Extreme Cold Alert level, a line made for people, the card says they are too cold for Goldens, as the walk ratings do.

What was left out, and why

Each of these is easy to find elsewhere. None of them holds up for dogs.

No pavement temperature

The pavement tables that circulate trace back to a 1970 report on three people burned on Phoenix asphalt: a study of people, not paws. Nobody has measured what surface temperature burns a dog's paw, so Dogs mode shows where the sun is instead of a number. The back-of-hand test is a fine habit; the published advice says 5, 7 or 10 seconds, and none of those counts was tested.

No ground temperature

Open-Meteo offers a ground-surface temperature. It is a model of a whole patch of the city, grass and trees and roofs as well as roads, not a reading of the asphalt your golden walks on, and no one has set a line for it. A bare number would invite a question nobody can answer, so it is left out.

No pollen count

Open-Meteo only has pollen for Europe, and the one study built to test pollen counts against itching in allergic dogs found no link. A golden walks with its nose in the grass; the count comes from a trap on a roof.

  • Open-Meteo air quality docs Strong
  • Widorn et al. 2024 Strong

No wind chill for dogs

The wind chill index models wind at the height of an adult's face and is built on a human face. A golden's double coat is exactly what it leaves out, so Dogs mode shows the air temperature and DC's legal line instead. It works the wind chill out in one place only: to tell when the forecast reaches DC's Extreme Cold Alert level, which the District writes in wind chill for people. Too cold for Goldens reads that level, and the number is never printed. Borrowing a line set for people for a golden is DC Goldens' choice, as it is for heat. Wind does take some warmth off the In the shade estimate, which is Vibe Temp's own line, not the wind chill.

  • NWS wind chill chart Strong
  • D.C. Code § 8-1801 Strong
  • DC Winter Plan FY26 Strong

No "too hot to walk" temperature

A chart saying to take care above about 20°C and not to walk a dog above 25°C is shared widely. DC Goldens could not trace it to any study. Most dog heatstroke follows exercise, usually an ordinary walk, so what a golden is doing matters alongside the thermometer, and one number can't carry that. Dogs mode shows the heat index, names it as a scale made for people, and puts the dog evidence beside it.

  • Hall et al. 2020, Animals Strong

Where you are

Vibe Temp works anywhere Open-Meteo has a forecast, and so does Dogs mode. But the levels it names are Washington, DC's: the Heat Alert at a heat index of 95°F and the Extreme Heat Alert at 105°F, the Extreme Cold Alert, and the D.C. Code's below-32°F line with its 15 minutes. Those are DC's levels, used as a reference wherever you are. Reaching one says the forecast is at that level; only DC can issue an alert, and only in DC. Nothing here means your local law limits a walk, and DC's doesn't either.

Thunderstorms come from the US National Weather Service, which forecasts only for the US. Outside the US there is no storm line, so no storm line there doesn't mean no storms. Inside the US, if the Weather Service's forecast doesn't load, the same holds: weather.gov has it in the meantime.

  28. Strong 2016

    Jordan, Bauer, Stella and Croney, Purdue University Center for Animal Welfare Science

    Cold tolerance varies widely by coat: the lower critical temperature is below 0°C (32°F) for a husky and 15°C (59°F) for some short-haired dogs. Housing numbers for dogs at rest, not on a walk.
